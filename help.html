Help: what this tool does, step by step

This page explains everything in the simplest words we can find. If you can read a picture book, you can follow it. Each step has a small picture in words.

1

What is this for?

Some gases are bad for the air. Cars, factories and power stations make them. We would like a material that grabs these gases and holds on, so they do not float away into the sky.

This tool tells you how well different metals can grab three gases: carbon dioxide (CO₂), carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂).

Picture it: a sticky hand. Some metals are very sticky. Some are not sticky at all. We measure the stickiness.

2

What is a metal, and what is a gas?

Everything is made of tiny, tiny balls called atoms. They are far too small to see. A piece of metal is millions and millions of the same ball, stacked neatly like marbles in a box. We use a flat top layer of that stack and call it the surface.

A gas is a little group of balls that is flying around in the air. CO₂ has one carbon ball in the middle and two oxygen balls on the sides. SO₂ has one sulfur ball with two oxygen balls, bent like a letter V.

Picture it: a marble wall (the metal) and a few tiny insects (the gas) flying toward it.

3

Does it stick or bounce?

When the gas touches the wall, one of two things happens. It sticks, or it bounces away. We find out by asking: is the gas happier on the wall, or happier in the air?

We write the answer as one number called the adsorption energy. It is measured in eV, short for electronvolt, which is just a tiny unit of energy.

Picture it: a ball and a bowl. If the ball rolls into the bowl and stays, it is happy there. That is a negative number, and the gas sticks. If the ball is sitting on top of a hill, it rolls off. That is a positive number, and the gas does not stick. The more negative the number, the deeper the bowl, and the harder it is to pull the gas off.

4

How do we get the number?

We ask a computer to work it out. We cannot measure the stickiness of every metal in a lab, because that takes a long time and costs a lot. The computer program is called Quantum ESPRESSO. It pretends to be all the tiny electrons that hold the atoms together and finds the energy.

The computer does three little sums, and each sum gives an energy:

  1. The wall with the gas just above it.
  2. The wall by itself.
  3. The gas by itself, in an empty box.

Then it takes the first energy and subtracts the other two. What is left over is how much the wall and the gas like each other. That is the adsorption energy.

Picture it: weigh the wall and the toy together. Then weigh the wall alone, and the toy alone. If the first is lighter than the other two added up, they like being together.

5

How to use the assistant

  1. Go to the Assistant page.
  2. Step 1: tap a metal in the little periodic table. Tiles with a dashed edge only get a rough guess.
  3. Step 2: look at the metal surface. Drag it to turn it around. Use the Top and Side buttons to see it from above or from the side. Tap one of the ringed atoms on top to say where the gas lands.
  4. Step 3: tap a gas. Each little molecule turns slowly so you can see its shape.
  5. Step 4 opens by itself. You see the gas floating above the metal, just like in the computer sum. Prefer typing? Use the box at the top and write a question in plain words.
  6. Read the big number. A negative number means the gas sticks.
  7. Read What this means. It says the same thing in a few easy sentences.
  8. Look at the bar picture. It shows your metal next to the other eleven. Blue bars stick, orange bars do not.
  9. If you want to repeat the calculation yourself, download the files under Simulation inputs.

6

What happens inside when you press Ask

Here is the logic, in the order it happens.

  1. Read. A reader picks out the metal and the gas from your words. If a language model is switched on, it does the reading. If not, a simple built in reader looks for names such as calcium or the symbol Ca. The page always tells you which one read your question.
  2. Look up. The tool opens its notebook of finished calculations and finds your metal and gas.
  3. Guess, only if needed. If your metal is not in the notebook, a small model makes a rough guess from two facts about the metal, how much it pulls on electrons and how big its atoms are. The page marks guesses clearly and says how wrong such guesses usually are. Like guessing a new child's height from the heights of the other children.
  4. Write the recipe cards. For calculated metals, the tool gives you the three input files, which are the recipe cards the computer reads, plus a small script that runs them in order and does the subtraction.
  5. Explain. The tool writes a short explanation that uses only the numbers it found. It does not make up new numbers.
7

How much should you trust a number?

  • Calculated numbers are the most reliable thing on this site, but they are still approximations. A difference smaller than about 0.05 eV is too small to tell apart.
  • Guesses from the small model are only a rough guide. The Results page shows how often they miss. For CO₂ the guess is fairly good. For CO and SO₂ it is poor.
  • A warning triangle means a number is still being checked, because it looks unusual.
8

What this tool cannot do yet

  • It covers twelve metals and three gases only.
  • The metals are clean, flat surfaces. Real metals outside a laboratory are usually covered by a thin crust of oxide, which changes how gases stick.
  • The gas was placed at a fixed height above one spot and was not allowed to move closer to its best position, so the numbers are not fully relaxed.
  • The calculations leave out the weak pull between all atoms called dispersion. Adding it usually makes binding look stronger.
  • It does not yet cover porous materials such as the MOFs used to capture gases in practice. That is the next step of the project.

Words you might meet

Adsorption
A gas sticking to the outside of a material. It is not the same as absorption, where it soaks inside.
eV (electronvolt)
A very small unit of energy, used for atoms. About 96.5 kJ per mol.
kJ per mol
Energy for a big pile of molecules at once. This is the unit most laboratory measurements use.
Å (angstrom)
A tiny length. One angstrom is one ten billionth of a metre. Atoms are about one to three angstroms wide.
DFT (density functional theory)
The method the computer uses to find the energy from the electrons.
PBE
A common rule of thumb inside DFT for how electrons push on each other.
Slab
A thin flat piece of metal used to stand for a surface, with empty space above and below it.
Cutoff (Ry)
How fine the computer's description of the electrons is. Higher is more careful and slower. Ry is a unit of energy, the Rydberg.
k points
How many places in the repeating piece of metal the computer checks. More points is more careful and slower.
Input file
The recipe card the computer reads: which atoms, where they sit, and how carefully to work.
